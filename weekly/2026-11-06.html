Editor's Note

This Week

Security teams like binary answers. A system is patched or it is not. An application has permission or it does not. This week's most interesting stories involve the places where the binary answer was true and still failed to describe the risk.

In one case, attackers reached root on an appliance that had every available update. In another, a widely reported attack turned out to have used a different flaw than the one defenders had been told to fix. In a third, Microsoft is changing a quiet default that let one kind of software agent create others, and its own documentation had called that default permanent. The common question is what an affirmative status was actually measuring.

This edition resumes after a gap, using Oct. 30 through Nov. 6 as its nominal seven-day selection window, with older material for context. Research closed Nov. 6 at 11:30 a.m. Mountain Time. Allow about 12 minutes for the complete issue.

01

Cybersecurity · Vulnerabilities · Attribution

Patched Is a Claim, Not a Condition

Ivanti, Zyxel and Apple each supplied a different version of the same problem: the status of one bug says little about the exposure that remains.

The Weekly Review · Approximately 6 minutes

The most unsettling detail in this week's Ivanti story is the one a vulnerability scanner would not have noticed. According to the security firm Volexity, as reported by SecurityWeek, attackers chained three flaws in Ivanti Connect Secure to get root access on an appliance that was fully patched at the time. Each step was an ordinary kind of bug. The result was not ordinary.

Ivanti disclosed the first flaw, CVE-2026-104935, an authentication bypass, in an Oct. 13 advisory. Two more were fixed this week: CVE-2026-111357, which lets an authenticated attacker with local access escalate privileges, and CVE-2026-118527, which lets an authenticated attacker upload files to a predictable location. SecurityWeek reports that the second has a severity score of only 4.3. A team ranking its work by score alone would reasonably have put it near the bottom of the list. In this chain it was one of the links that carried the attacker to root.

The practical consequence is a second upgrade. Ivanti's earlier fix for the authentication bypass shipped in versions 25.1.1.2 and 22.8R2.5. The fixes for the whole chain are in 25.1.1.3 and 22.8R2.6, so an administrator who finished the October work has more to do. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) added both new flaws to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog on Oct. 30. The federal deadline for CVE-2026-111357 was Nov. 2, which has already passed. The deadline for CVE-2026-118527 is Nov. 13.

The Clean Result Problem

Here the reporting is thinner, and it should be read that way. A summary from CISO Platform says Volexity found no web shell or file left on disk, with commands and results carried inside the chain's own web requests. It also says the activity evades Ivanti's Integrity Checker Tool. I could not read Volexity's original analysis directly, so those details remain a secondhand account until someone checks them against it.

If they hold, the implication reaches beyond Ivanti. A scan that finds nothing is evidence about the things the scan looks for. It is weak evidence about an attacker who has chosen not to leave those things behind. Volexity, according to SecurityWeek, tracks the actor as UTA0576, a previously unknown group, and assesses with low confidence that it is criminally motivated. Ivanti has not named an actor, and it says a limited number of customers were affected before disclosure. Ivanti also said the root-level bug had no fix when the attacks surfaced in mid-October.

The Wrong Bug

Zyxel supplies the quieter lesson. In September, the threat-intelligence firm GreyNoise reported roughly 1,000 compromised GS1900 switches and tied the attacks to CVE-2026-7273, a flaw Zyxel had patched in June. CISA added that flaw to the KEV catalog. SecurityWeek reported on Nov. 5 that the firmware security firm Binarly now says the attackers used a different, previously unknown flaw, which Zyxel has since fixed as CVE-2026-107441.

Binarly reported the problem to Zyxel on Sept. 29, and the firmware fix was released Oct. 23. Its sharpest claim is that the earlier patch "remained ineffective," meaning that organizations that did what the public guidance told them to do may not have been protected. That is Binarly's assessment. The SecurityWeek article does not include a response from Zyxel, and the claim should be held at that level until it does.

Consider the position of an administrator. The public narrative said a patched flaw was behind the attacks. The administrator applied the patch and reasonably recorded the matter as closed. The record was correct about the patch and wrong about the exposure. Nobody did anything careless. An attribution made from the outside, reasonably and in good faith, had pointed to the wrong bug.

The Old Fix, Still Working for the Attacker

Apple's case is the opposite. The flaw is real, old and fixed. The Hacker News reported on Nov. 5 that CISA added CVE-2024-27834 to the KEV catalog. Apple fixed the Pointer Authentication bypass in May 2024, and the article notes it was first demonstrated at Pwn2Own Vancouver two months earlier. The article describes a zero-click chain reaching victims through compromised and fake websites and a supply-chain attack on an SEO tracking tool, and it reports that the security firm Sekoia attributes the campaign to the Russian-linked group APT29 with moderate-to-high confidence.

The attackers did not need a new vulnerability. They needed people who had not applied an old update. Federal civilian agencies have until Nov. 18 to patch. The article's chain description contains what appears to be a typographical error, naming one CVE twice, so the technical specifics deserve confirmation against the Sekoia report before anyone repeats them.

The record was correct about the patch and wrong about the exposure.

What a Status Is Worth

Set the three side by side and a pattern appears, though it is a modest one. Ivanti shows that a fully patched system can still be compromised through a combination of flaws that no single advisory describes. Zyxel shows that attribution can send defenders to the wrong fix. Apple shows that a fixed flaw remains a live weapon against anyone who has not caught up. In each, a status such as "patched," "not exploited" or "clean" answered a narrower question than the one an executive hoped it answered.

The counterargument is worth stating. Three incidents chosen from a week of reporting are an anecdote, not a trend, and every one of them depends partly on a single vendor or researcher's account. Patching old flaws remains the highest-value routine work in the field, and the Apple case is a plain reminder of that. Nothing here argues for abandoning severity scores or the KEV catalog. It argues for treating them as inputs.

The sturdier habit is to ask what a status was based on. Which flaw does the patch address, and who confirmed the attacker used that one? What did the scan look for, and what would an attacker who avoided those things look like? A team that can answer those questions can say how much confidence its clean record deserves, and which assumptions to revisit when a vendor revises the story.

Sources and limits: SecurityWeek on Ivanti, CISO Platform's summary, SecurityWeek on Zyxel and The Hacker News on Apple. The Volexity and Sekoia findings, and Binarly's claim about the earlier Zyxel patch, are reported secondhand here and are not independently confirmed. Vendor advisories were not read directly. The synthesis across the three cases is editorial analysis.

02

Microsoft · Enterprise Identity · AI Agents

The Permission Nobody Granted

Entra gave a certain kind of AI agent the power to create other agents by default. This month, Microsoft is making that power something an administrator must choose to give.

The Weekly Review · Approximately 5 minutes

Microsoft's documentation for Entra Agent ID contains a sentence that reads strangely next to this week's news. A permission called AgentIdentity.CreateAsManager, the creation-channels page says, "is automatically granted to any agent identity blueprint principal in the tenant." It also says the permission "can't be revoked."

The message-center notice that arrived in October describes a different world. According to MC1506728, starting Nov. 4 Entra stops granting that permission automatically. An administrator must grant it, and the notice says the change makes the authority "visible, auditable, and revocable." The Learn page, as I could read it, still describes the old arrangement and appears to predate the change. The notice, posted Oct. 13, is the newer and more specific source.

Two cautions apply at once. I read the notice on a public mirror of Microsoft's message center, not inside a tenant, so dates and wording should be confirmed in the admin portal before anyone acts. And the notice says the rollout begins in early November and finishes by late November, so a given tenant may not have the new behavior yet.

Agents, Blueprints and the People Behind Them

The vocabulary is new enough to need a short explanation. In Entra Agent ID, an agent identity is the account an AI agent uses. It is created from a blueprint, a template, and the blueprint has a tenant-side principal that can be given privileges. The automatic permission let that principal create agent identities, and update and delete the ones it created, up to a limit of 250.

Microsoft's ownership documentation is more interesting than the permission, because it shows how the company thinks about accountability. It separates three relationships. Owners are technical administrators responsible for setup and credentials. Sponsors are business representatives accountable for the agent's purpose and for decisions such as access reviews and retention, and each agent identity needs at least one. Managers sit in the organization's hierarchy and can request access packages for their agents, but cannot modify or delete them.

Read together, the two documents describe a design intent: someone technical should run each agent, and someone accountable should be able to say why it exists. Automatic, irrevocable authority to create more agents sits awkwardly with that intent, because a creation made by software acting alone is hard to trace to a person who chose it. For app-only requests, the Learn page says the creating service must itself name a sponsor, so the rule exists at the point of creation as well.

Owner

Technical administrator responsible for setup, configuration and credentials.

Sponsor

Business representative accountable for purpose, access reviews and retention.

Manager

Person in the reporting chain who can request access for the agent, with no power to change it.

What Could Break

The immediate effect is narrow. The notice says that an app-only request, one made with no signed-in user, that relied solely on the automatic grant will receive a 403 authorization error once enforcement reaches the tenant. It says the change affects existing and future agents, does not delete existing agent identities or blueprints, and does not affect agents authorized through a directory role. Organizations with no agents creating other agents need do nothing.

For those that do, the notice offers a method. Administrators can search the Entra audit logs for service principals added by an application and aimed at an agent identity, then check each blueprint's granted permissions for any other permission that allows creation. If none exists, the agent depends on the automatic grant. Administrators can grant the permission explicitly through tenant-wide consent or a manual app role, which the notice says has been possible since Oct. 16, and test before enforcement arrives.

This is the operational kernel of the story, and it resembles the Oct. 2 edition's lesson from the Exchange retirement. A permission that was never requested has no owner and no expiration, and nobody remembers approving it because nobody did. Converting it to an explicit grant creates a record: a named administrator, a date and a place in the audit log. That is a more useful security property than the convenience it replaces.

The Case for the Old Default

The old design had a logic. An agent platform that required human approval for every identity an agent spawned would slow exactly the automation it was built to support, and Microsoft limited the damage with a cap of 250 creations. The change may therefore cost some teams a few hours of testing and an administrator's attention. Microsoft says no specific compliance effects have been identified.

The better question concerns what the change signals. A platform that made autonomous creation easy in its first months and is now making it explicit has decided that "visible, auditable, and revocable" matters more than frictionless. That is a sensible direction. It also suggests that early agent deployments were granted broader authority than their owners may have realized, and that the inventory of what these agents can do is worth taking while the platform is still young.

Sources and limits: MC1506728 on a public mirror, a third-party copy of Microsoft's message center that should be checked in the tenant; and Microsoft Learn pages on agent creation channels, owners, sponsors and managers and creating and deleting agent identities. The Learn page on creation channels conflicts with the notice and appears to be out of date. The inference about early agent deployments is editorial analysis.

Selected Developments

The Week in Brief

Economy

Hiring Rebounds, and September Gets Revised

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that payroll employment rose by 165,000 in October, and the unemployment rate was little changed at 4.1%. The same release revised September up from 29,000 to 60,000 and August from 133,000 to 186,000, adding 84,000 jobs. That changes the picture the Oct. 2 edition described as a small gain. The unemployment rate has stayed between 4.1% and 4.3% since March. One report, even a strong one, does not settle the trend.

Courts and AI

The Judiciary's AI Guidance Is Still Coming

This story predates the week, but it is worth recording. Judge Sidney Thomas of the Ninth Circuit, who chairs the federal judiciary's AI task force, said in September that the group aims to share final guidance by the end of the year, according to the ABA Journal. He noted that the more significant use may be in court operations rather than chambers. PYMNTS, citing Reuters, reports about 30 recommendations so far and a concern that judges might become overly dependent on generative AI. The recommendations have not been published.

Microsoft

A Sign-In Domain Moves Later This Month

Third-party message-center summaries of MC1462460 describe a late-November move of Entra's My Account and self-service pages to myaccount.cloud.microsoft. Administrators are advised to allow the cloud.microsoft domains in network policies. I did not inspect the authoritative notice, so confirm the dates and scope in the tenant before changing filtering rules.
