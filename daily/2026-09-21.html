Security + AI

The Lead · Frontier AI Evaluation

More access can improve AI oversight. It can also blur the evaluator’s line.

Anthropic’s proposed embedded-evaluation partnership with Accenture is potentially valuable because external reviewers often lack the information needed to assess a frontier system. The design should be judged by its practical independence and public record.

Published September 18 · 4-minute read

Anthropic says it will partner with Accenture’s Faculty business to place independent evaluators inside the company, with access comparable to an employee’s. The firms say the work will include red-teaming, alignment assessments and testing of model safeguards, and that each expects to invest at least $1 billion over five years in building the capacity.

The attraction is straightforward. Traditional outside evaluation is often limited to a model snapshot, a short testing window and whatever information the developer chooses to provide. An embedded team could observe how a system is trained, how safeguards change, how failures are investigated and whether mitigations persist after a launch deadline approaches. That kind of access can make an assessment more useful than a polished public summary.

Access alone is not independence. Anthropic says details remain under development. The eventual arrangement needs clear rules on what evaluators may test, whether they can report unfavorable findings without approval, who pays for the work, and what gets published when the two parties disagree. An evaluator who can see everything but cannot say much has limited public value.

For organizations buying or governing advanced AI, the announcement is a useful prompt to ask similar questions of their own assurance process. A vendor attestation, a one-time red-team report and a live operational review answer different questions. The closer a system is to consequential work, the more valuable it is to preserve evidence of testing, remediation and exceptions.

A Test Worth Watching

Look for the first published scope, methodology and findings. The strongest evidence would include limits on the evaluators’ access, unresolved risks and a visible path for dissent.

Enterprise Tech

Windows · Out-of-Band Update

A Credential Guard issue turns patching into an identity-health check

Microsoft’s Sept. 14 out-of-band update adds a protection for an elevation-of-privilege vulnerability and acknowledges a separate issue in which protected machine accounts can lose their secure channel with on-premises Active Directory.

Published September 14 · 3-minute read

The out-of-band Windows 11 update KB5129194 includes protections associated with CVE-2026-62721, a Windows User-Mode Power Service elevation-of-privilege vulnerability. More practically for many administrators, Microsoft says that after the Sept. 8 security update or a later update, some Credential Guard-protected machine accounts may lose their secure channel with an on-premises Active Directory domain.

A machine that loses that relationship may not fail in one dramatic way. The symptoms can arrive as authentication, management or policy problems that appear unrelated to a security update. Microsoft says it plans a future fix and is temporarily preventing Machine Identity Isolation enforcement while it improves the feature.

The sensible response is targeted verification, not a broad rollback. Teams using Credential Guard or Machine Identity Isolation should identify the affected device populations, monitor secure-channel and domain-trust errors, and test the out-of-band update in the same network conditions used by managed devices. Identity controls are valuable precisely because failures at this layer can be difficult to diagnose once users are affected.

AI Governance · Preview

Microsoft distinguishes Copilot controls from the wider AI estate

Microsoft’s documentation separates the Microsoft 365 Copilot security dashboard, which focuses on Copilot data protection, oversharing and compliance, from its Security Dashboard for AI preview. The latter combines Defender, Entra and Purview signals across Copilot, Foundry, third-party AI apps and agents. That split is useful only if ownership is equally clear: a dashboard does not resolve who accepts or remediates a discovered risk.

U.S. + World

International Security · Developing

The U.N. week begins with the costs of unresolved conflict already arriving

World leaders meet as Iran, Gaza, Ukraine and other wars compete for attention. Energy, shipping and inflation are the immediate channels through which those conflicts reach far beyond their borders.

Updated September 21 · 3-minute read

The annual United Nations gathering opens this week against a crowded agenda of wars, climate shocks, rising prices and concern about advanced artificial intelligence. Associated Press reports that leaders are expected to confront conflicts in Iran, Gaza, Ukraine, Sudan, Congo and Myanmar, while a U.S.-China meeting is also scheduled in Washington.

The test is not whether leaders can name the problems. It is whether diplomacy changes the conditions around them. The Iran war and constraints on shipping through the Strait of Hormuz have already turned a regional conflict into an energy and supply-chain problem. Ukraine’s weekend launch of more than 1,000 drones at Russia and Russia’s subsequent strike on Zaporizhzhia underline that the other major war is also escalating in ways that will shape the meetings.

For a daily reader, the most useful signal is concrete movement: a credible shipping arrangement, a change in sanctions enforcement, new military commitments or an agreed diplomatic process. General statements at the General Assembly are routine. Operational commitments, and whether the parties act on them, are what would change the risk picture.

Sources: Associated Press: U.N. agenda Associated Press: Zaporizhzhia strike Associated Press: Iran and Hormuz developments

Something Interesting

Earth Observation · Colorado Basin

Lake Powell’s record-low level makes the water problem visible from orbit

NASA links this year’s decline to snow drought and unusually warm temperatures. The striking image is useful because it turns a long-term basin problem into a current operational measure.

Published September 18 · 2-minute read

NASA’s Earth Observatory reports that Lake Powell fell to a record-low level in 2026 after a snow drought and unusually warm temperatures reduced water storage in the Colorado Basin. The reservoir’s changing shoreline is visible in satellite imagery, but the more consequential detail is its role in a system that supplies water and hydropower across the West.

A single reservoir level does not explain every allocation, legal obligation or future forecast. Still, it is a concrete indicator of the compound problem: less mountain snowpack, warmer conditions and demand that does not disappear when the inflow weakens. The story is a useful reminder that remote sensing can show not just a scientific curiosity, but the condition of infrastructure people rely on.

Source: NASA Earth Observatory: Lake Powell drops to record-low levels
