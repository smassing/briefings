The Lead · Active Exploitation

F5 BIG-IP APM zero-day requires an exposure check, a fix and an evidence review

CVE-2026-94127 is a critical heap-based buffer overflow in BIG-IP Access Policy Manager. It affects systems where an APM access policy and an OAuth profile are configured on the same virtual server.

Updated September 24 · 4-minute read

This is the kind of vulnerability notice that deserves a fast, structured response. The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security says F5 reported exploitation in the wild, and CERT-EU describes the flaw as allowing unauthenticated remote code execution. Not every BIG-IP deployment is necessarily exposed, but organizations should establish quickly whether their configuration meets the stated condition.

Start with inventory and configuration evidence. Identify the BIG-IP versions and virtual servers in scope, confirm whether the affected roles coexist, and map the appliances to their internet exposure and business functions. Apply the F5-provided hotfix or supported update on the vendor’s schedule. A maintenance window is still useful, but active exploitation is a reason to give this work priority over normal patch batching.

Patching addresses the vulnerable condition. It does not establish that an appliance was not already accessed. Retain and examine relevant BIG-IP, authentication and network logs, particularly for administrative changes, new accounts, configuration exports and unusual outbound traffic. Where logging is incomplete, document that limitation and consider a more conservative incident response.

The lasting lesson is familiar: internet-facing identity infrastructure is high-value infrastructure. A clear remediation owner, a tested update route and adequate log retention are what turn a vendor advisory into a defensible response.

Sources: Canadian Centre for Cyber Security alert · CERT-EU advisory

Courts + Law

Federal Judiciary · Technology Governance

The courts’ AI program starts with the right limit: authority stays with people

The Judicial Conference’s September update connects AI policy, case-management modernization and system stewardship. Its most important proposition is practical: judicial functions cannot be delegated to a tool.

Published September 17 · 3-minute read

The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts says its AI task force has identified more than 60 issues, prioritized them and formed seven subject-matter groups. Interim guidance is in place while the judiciary develops additional policy. The work reflects the reality that AI use arrives through administrative work, research, vendor systems and filings before every policy question has a settled answer.

The judiciary has drawn a clear boundary. AI may assist, but core judicial functions, including decision-making and case adjudication, remain human responsibilities. Users are accountable for the work their tools produce. That is more useful than a generic warning about hallucinations because it assigns responsibility at the point where authority is exercised.

The update also places AI work beside the Case Management/Electronic Case Files redesign. The pairing is sensible. Good AI governance depends on access controls, reliable records, training, security design and a way to examine how a result reached a user.

Source: U.S. Courts: Judicial Conference update

Windows 11

Credential Guard issue calls for a targeted post-update check

Microsoft says that after the Sept. 8 Windows security update, some Credential Guard-protected machine accounts can lose their secure channel with an on-premises Active Directory domain. The Sept. 22 preview update carries the same known-issue notice. Identify protected machine accounts, test in representative environments and have a recovery procedure before broad deployment.
