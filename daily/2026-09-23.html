The Lead · International Security

The New York meeting matters only if it produces a channel both sides can use

A lengthy U.S.-Iran meeting during the General Assembly is a real diplomatic event after months of war. It is not yet evidence that the conflict has an agreed off-ramp.

Updated September 23 · 4-minute read

The U.N. General Assembly’s second day has put Iran and Ukraine at the center of the agenda, but the most consequential reported development is off the podium. The National reported that U.S. and Iranian officials held a lengthy meeting in New York while both sides sought a way through their deadlock. Public reporting has not established an agreement, a timetable or mutually accepted terms.

That distinction is worth keeping. Yesterday’s addresses showed how far apart the public positions remain. President Donald Trump defended the war and described an impending choice between a deal and much greater escalation. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian arrived in New York accusing Washington of trying to force Iran’s surrender. A meeting can coexist with those positions, but it does not erase them.

For the conflict’s wider effects, the useful questions are operational. Is there a channel that can keep working after the General Assembly? Are Gulf states or another party acting as an intermediary? Does either side accept near-term steps that reduce risk to shipping, energy infrastructure or civilians while a broader political question remains unsettled? A public promise without those features would be hard to assess and easy to reverse.

The Assembly remains useful even when it cannot compel an outcome. It gathers the parties, exposes which issues have become international priorities and creates room for side meetings. Its record should be measured against concrete changes in the conflict, however, rather than the temperature of the speeches.

Identity Security · NIST Guidance

An access token is a credential with a shorter biography, not a smaller risk

NIST’s finalized guidance on protecting online identity and access tokens offers a timely reminder: once a bearer credential is exposed, the original sign-in control may no longer be relevant to the attacker.

Published September 15 · 3-minute read

Tokens are ordinary plumbing in modern systems. Applications use them to call services, automated workflows use them to avoid repeated human sign-in, and browsers retain them to keep sessions usable. The same convenience creates a familiar security condition: someone who obtains a valid bearer token may be able to act as its holder until it expires or is revoked.

NIST’s finalized token guidance points toward lifecycle controls rather than a single technical fix. High-value tokens should have narrow scopes and short useful lives where the workflow permits. They need protected storage, a reliable revocation path, disciplined logging and detection capable of noticing use from an unexpected place or by an unexpected process. Teams also need to know which service owns a token before an incident makes that question urgent.

Agentic workflows raise the stakes because credentials can move through a chain of tools, services and delegated tasks. Giving an agent a token with broad, long-lived authority may be expedient during a pilot, but it creates a recovery problem later. The safer operating model is task-bound authority, visible approvals and the ability to terminate a session without disabling an entire platform.

A Focused Review

Choose one high-value automation this week. Identify its token, scope, lifetime, storage location, audit trail and revocation owner. If any answer is unclear, the control is not ready to be depended on during an incident.

Agent Security · NIST

Identity and authorization remain the unglamorous core of agent safety

NIST’s software-agent identity project focuses on identification, authorization, auditing and nonrepudiation. That is a useful corrective to treating agents as merely better chat interfaces. An agent that can read data or invoke tools needs a clear principal, bounded permission and evidence of what it did. Capability without those controls produces an accountability gap.

Courts + Law

Federal Judiciary · Technology Governance

The courts’ AI program starts with the right limit: authority stays with people

The Judicial Conference’s September update connects AI policy, case-management modernization and system stewardship. Its most important current proposition is practical rather than novel: judicial functions cannot be delegated to a tool.

Published September 17 · 3-minute read

The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts reports that its AI task force has identified more than 60 issues, prioritized them and formed seven subject-matter groups. Interim guidance is in place while the judiciary develops further policy. The work reflects a reality most institutions face: AI use will arrive through administrative work, research, vendor systems and filings long before every policy question has a settled answer.

The judiciary has drawn a clear boundary. AI may assist, but core judicial functions, including decision-making and case adjudication, remain human responsibilities. Users are accountable for the work their tools produce. This is a stronger operating rule than a generic warning about hallucinations because it assigns responsibility at the point where authority is exercised.

The update also places AI work beside the Case Management/Electronic Case Files redesign, whose first component is expected before year-end. That pairing is sensible. Good AI governance depends on the surrounding system: access controls, reliable records, training, security design and a way to examine how a result reached a user. A policy that ignores those conditions is mostly an aspiration.

Something Interesting

AI Security · Research

The case for continuous AI security has a mathematical edge

A NIST researcher argues that no finite set of guardrails can be universally robust against adaptive adversarial prompts. The useful conclusion is operational: security work on AI is a continuing practice, not a certification event.

Published June 9 · 2-minute read

NIST’s work frames an uncomfortable truth in unusually direct terms. If an AI system accepts rich natural-language input, attackers can keep looking for ways to express harmful intent that a fixed safety layer fails to recognize. The research does not mean controls are futile. It means a one-time assurance claim should be treated with skepticism.

The practical response is familiar to security teams: red-team the system, update defenses as weaknesses are found, limit the harm an exploit can cause and recover quickly. That is less glamorous than declaring a model safe, but it is closer to how resilient systems are actually maintained.

Source: NIST: continuous monitoring and updating for AI systems
