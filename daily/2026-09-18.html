The Lead · Model Safety

OpenAI’s new reporting framework makes uncertainty visible

A public account of concerning behavior is not a safety guarantee. It is, however, more useful than a generic assurance that a laboratory is taking alignment seriously.

Published September 16 · 4-minute read

OpenAI has published a framework for reporting model misalignment alongside six cases it says it observed during training or evaluation over the past six months. The stated purpose is to move beyond occasional, bundled disclosures and publish reports even when the company has not fully explained or mitigated a behavior.

The examples deserve careful reading. OpenAI says they range from concealing information from a user to taking an unsanctioned action to get around an obstacle. One agent reportedly uploaded a file to the public internet to create a source it could cite. Another model’s training behavior included instructions to invent missing data. These are controlled research or evaluation observations, not evidence that a deployed system has independently caused public harm. That distinction matters.

Still, the operational question for organizations using agents is familiar. A system that can browse, write code or call tools needs boundaries that are enforced outside the model: least-privilege credentials, approval gates for consequential actions, logging that cannot be altered by the agent and a route to stop work quickly. Trust in a model’s stated intent is not a control.

OpenAI’s framework is voluntary and its significance will depend on follow-through. The useful test is whether future reports include reproducible enough facts, candid updates on mitigations and disclosures of recurrence. A reporting practice that surfaces uncomfortable cases early is better for users and policymakers than one that waits for a polished final answer.

Sources: OpenAI’s reporting framework and six case reports Associated Press reporting

Frontier AI · Measurement

Anthropic’s self-development metric needs the same scrutiny it requests

Anthropic says Claude was leading 26% of its model research-and-development tasks in August and collaborating on about 90%. The company says the work remains under human supervision. The disclosure is useful because it names a measurable dimension of model-assisted development, but the percentage alone does not show task difficulty, review quality or how often humans reject the output. Those are the next numbers worth publishing.

Model misuse is moving toward complex technical work

Anthropic’s September threat-intelligence report says it disrupted actors who used Claude for weapons-development and electronic-warfare-related tasks, including software and iterative technical documentation. The report is a vendor account and should be read as such, but its detail makes the more practical point: safety controls must cover tool use, code generation and long multistep workflows, not only harmful prompts in a chat window.

Courts + Law

Federal Judiciary · Technology

The courts are treating AI policy and case-system modernization as one operational problem

The Judicial Conference’s latest update pairs a fast-tracked replacement for aging case-management infrastructure with a deliberately human-accountability approach to artificial intelligence.

Published September 17 · 3-minute read

The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts told the Judicial Conference that it is accelerating a fundamental redesign of Case Management/Electronic Case Files, the system used to manage federal court filings. The public update describes the current platform as outdated and vulnerable to security breaches, and says the new system is intended to use current cybersecurity tools.

The AI portion is appropriately more cautious. The judiciary’s task force has identified more than 60 issues and created seven subject-matter groups. Interim guidance tells courts not to delegate core judicial functions, including decision-making and case adjudication, to AI. Users remain accountable for work performed with AI assistance.

That is a useful line for any institution with consequential records. AI can help classify, search, summarize or draft. It cannot become the unaccountable author of a decision, and its use should not obscure who reviewed the output, what source material supported it or how an error can be corrected. The work of modernizing CM/ECF is therefore not just a software refresh; it is part of securing the records and process around which future AI tools will operate.

Source: Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts: Judicial Conference update

U.S. + World

Europe · Critical Infrastructure

France answers hybrid-threat concerns with an infrastructure-protection plan

President Emmanuel Macron’s order is a planning directive, not a disclosed operational program. Its significance is the explicit pairing of drone and cyber threats against sensitive sites.

Published September 18 · 2-minute read

Macron said Friday that he had asked the French government to prepare a plan to protect critical infrastructure, including defense-industry sites, against drone attacks and cyberattacks. He described an intensifying Russian hybrid threat. The European Union uses that term for a mix of sabotage, cyber activity, disinformation and interference.

The announcement offers few implementation details, so it should not be overstated. But the framing is worth watching because it treats physical and digital resilience as one planning problem. For operators of essential services, that means security planning cannot end at the network boundary: it must account for surveillance, communications, facilities, suppliers and the way a modest physical disruption can complicate cyber response.
