Artificial Intelligence · Investigation

OpenAI traces a Russian influence operation beyond the chatbot

The most useful lesson is architectural: generative text was only one layer in a campaign that borrowed scholarship, invented institutional credibility and tried to hide who was operating it.

Disclosed Aug. 25 · 4-minute read

OpenAI says it banned a cluster of ChatGPT accounts originating in Russia after an investigation connected them to a broader covert influence campaign promoting the International Burke Institute, a self-described expert organization presented as Israeli. The company says the operation used AI-generated social posts, but the surrounding machinery mattered more: a website containing copied and misattributed academic work, a sovereignty index favorable to Russia and efforts to conceal the operators' origins.

The disclosure is a useful correction to the idea that AI-enabled influence is mainly a volume problem. Cheap text generation can help populate accounts and adapt messages, but a convincing operation still depends on assets that look independent: institutions, experts, research products, citations and an origin story. Those are the pieces likely to survive a simple detector trained to spot synthetic prose.

OpenAI's account is also necessarily partial. It describes activity visible on its own platform and actions the company took against associated accounts. It does not, by itself, establish how many people encountered the campaign or whether it changed anyone's views. The distinction between capability, activity and measured impact remains important when evaluating influence claims.

For defenders, the investigation points toward entity-level analysis. A suspicious post is more informative when examined alongside domain registration, author biographies, reused documents, image provenance, citation patterns, account coordination and the history of the organization claiming authority. Generative AI makes artifacts cheaper. It does not make the surrounding identity infrastructure irrelevant.

Defensive lesson

Teams monitoring influence or impersonation should preserve the relationships among accounts, domains, documents and claimed experts. Looking only for AI-written sentences misses the system that gives those sentences credibility.

Cybersecurity · Identity

A voice-phishing attack again puts help-desk controls on trial

The attackers reportedly used calls and lookalike single sign-on infrastructure against a security company, reinforcing that identity recovery remains part of the attack surface.

Reported Aug. 24 · 3-minute read

Health Information Sharing and Analysis Center reporting describes an Aug. 22 campaign linked to ShinyHunters that targeted ReliaQuest through voice phishing and lookalike single sign-on infrastructure. The method follows the identity-first playbook associated with ShinyHunters and the overlapping Scattered Spider ecosystem: persuade a person with access to help the attacker cross the authentication boundary, then work through software-as-a-service systems rather than drop conventional malware first.

The name of the victim is noteworthy because mature security teams are not immune to the basic asymmetry of a convincing call. An attacker can choose the time, gather public details and repeat the attempt. A support worker must distinguish the real employee from the rehearsed impostor while trying to restore access quickly.

Controls that depend on the same compromised channel are weak recovery controls. A caller who claims a phone problem should not be able to use that phone call as the primary evidence needed to reset multifactor authentication. High-risk resets should require a separate, trusted verification path, should produce an alert and should leave enough telemetry to reconstruct who approved what.

The practical review is narrow and testable: who may reset authentication methods, what evidence they require, whether caller ID influences the decision, how privileged users are treated, whether a new device triggers session revocation, and how quickly security sees the change. A tabletop call to the help desk may reveal more than another general awareness module.

Check now

Review multifactor-authentication reset and account-recovery procedures for privileged and high-value accounts. Require an out-of-band verification step that does not rely on information supplied during the incoming call.

Supreme Court · Emergency Docket

Court clears mail-voting order to proceed, but leaves legality unresolved

The 6-3 order pauses a lower-court injunction because the states' claimed injuries were considered premature. It does not finally approve the administration's planned election changes.

Decided Aug. 24 · 4-minute read

The Supreme Court has allowed the Trump administration to move forward, for now, with an executive order directing federal agencies to develop new restrictions and procedures around mail-in voting. The unsigned majority put on hold a Massachusetts federal judge's injunction that had protected 23 states and the District of Columbia while their challenge proceeds.

The immediate dispute concerned three directives. One tells the Department of Homeland Security to create and send states lists of adult citizens. Another tells the attorney general to prioritize investigations and prosecutions involving ballots issued to ineligible voters. A third directs the U.S. Postal Service toward a rule involving voter lists, envelope standards and unique barcodes for federal ballot mail.

The majority concluded that the states lacked a sufficiently concrete injury when they filed. It characterized portions of the order as internal instructions whose implementation remained uncertain. If a final Postal Service rule later harms the states, the court said, they may challenge that rule. The government also persuaded the majority that leaving the injunction in place could prevent implementation before the November election.

The line between procedure and substance is crucial. The court did not hold that the president has constitutional or statutory authority for every eventual measure. Justice Sonia Sotomayor, joined by Justice Elena Kagan, emphasized that point even while dissenting from the stay. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson wrote separately in dissent and warned of disruption close to the election.

A separate injunction obtained by other plaintiffs still restricts the Postal Service from implementing one provision nationwide. The litigation is therefore moving on several tracks, and the operational picture may change again as agencies publish concrete rules or guidance.

What to watch

The next legally meaningful documents may be agency implementation measures rather than another broad order. Concrete voter-list, mailing or enforcement requirements will sharpen standing, statutory-authority and federalism questions.

Enterprise Tech

Microsoft 365 · Administration

Microsoft centralizes agent oversight across customer tenants

The public preview gives multi-tenant administrators a consolidated inventory and control surface, but deeper activity and risk insight depends on Agent 365 licensing.

Current in August · 3-minute read

Microsoft has placed multi-tenant agent management into public preview in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Cloud Solution Provider partners, systems integrators and organizations operating subsidiary tenants can view a consolidated agent inventory, install or block agents in eligible tenants, review tenant-specific information and switch into the governed tenant without maintaining separate sessions.

The design responds to a predictable administrative gap. Agents may arrive through Copilot Studio, vendor products or local development faster than central teams can inventory their permissions and owners. A tenant-by-tenant view makes that problem harder for managed service providers and decentralized enterprises, where the same governance question repeats across separate directories.

Actions remain limited by the administrator's delegated role through Granular Delegated Admin Privileges. Microsoft also notes that viewing an end user's agent activity and risky-agent insights requires an Agent 365 license for that user. That creates a distinction administrators should capture early: basic inventory and control may be available, while evidence needed for behavior-level governance may carry a per-user licensing dependency.

A consolidated screen is not a governance program by itself. The useful operating questions remain who approves an agent, which identity it uses, what data and tools it can reach, how its activity is logged, when access expires and who owns removal. The new view should make those questions easier to enforce consistently across tenants.

Administrative consequence

Organizations managing multiple tenants should test the preview against their delegated-role model and document which risk fields disappear without Agent 365. That licensing boundary may affect both rollout cost and audit evidence.

U.S. + World

The Lead · Developing

Washington opens a wider economic front against Iran

The new sanctions package reaches procurement, oil smuggling, shipping, technology and finance. Its effect will depend on how aggressively the United States pressures third countries and companies.

Announced Aug. 24 · 4-minute read

The Trump administration has launched what Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent calls Operation Economic Outcast, an effort to isolate Iran by expanding secondary-sanctions pressure on countries and companies that continue doing business with it. Treasury also sanctioned nearly 60 entities tied to nuclear and missile procurement, cyber operations and oil smuggling, while revoking licenses that had permitted some payments and institutional access.

The material development since last week's promised economic campaign is breadth. Aviation, digital assets, gold, shipping and technology now sit alongside oil in the stated enforcement frame. Secondary sanctions can matter because they force firms outside the United States to choose between Iranian business and access to U.S. finance or markets.

The administration has not yet identified every target or explained how exceptions, humanitarian channels and allied coordination will work. That uncertainty is consequential. A broad announcement may chill legitimate transactions before formal enforcement begins, while uneven implementation can shift trade through intermediaries without stopping it.

Iran says it is prepared for the pressure and has threatened U.S. interests and energy chokepoints if its infrastructure is attacked. Its Strait of Hormuz authority also blacklisted 45 tankers for alleged rule violations. Those moves keep the economic and military fronts connected: shipping risk, insurance, crude prices and inflation can respond to perceived escalation before sanctions show measurable effects on Iranian revenue.

Pakistan, meanwhile, says talks in Tehran made significant progress toward a path to peace. That diplomatic claim remains thinly specified, but it is one of the few countervailing signals in a cycle otherwise dominated by economic coercion and threats.

What changed

The United States moved from promising additional pressure to naming a cross-sector campaign and a large first tranche of targets. The next test is enforcement against third-country firms and the resulting effect on shipping and energy prices.

Diplomacy · Syria

United States removes Syria from state-sponsor list

The decision removes a major political barrier for Syria's new government, but banking access and investment will depend on the wider sanctions and compliance environment.

Announced Aug. 24 · 2-minute read

The United States has removed Syria from its list of state sponsors of terrorism, citing commitments by President Ahmed al-Sharaa's government to distance the country from terrorism. Syrian officials called the designation the last major obstacle to reconnecting with the international financial system.

Removal is consequential but not self-executing economic normalization. Banks and companies will examine remaining sanctions, ownership risks, anti-money-laundering obligations and the government's actual conduct before restoring relationships. Years of isolation also leave damaged institutions and infrastructure that a designation change cannot repair quickly.

The decision nevertheless changes the diplomatic baseline. Washington is signaling that the new Syrian government can earn a different relationship through behavior and commitments. That creates leverage, but it also raises the cost if the government later fails to meet the conditions that justified removal.

Technology Controls · Taiwan

An AI-server export case moves enforcement inside the supply chain

Taiwanese prosecutors have indicted nine people, including employees of Nvidia and Super Micro, over alleged illegal exports of high-end servers to China.

Reported Aug. 25 · 3-minute read

Taiwanese prosecutors say nine people have been indicted for breach of trust and document forgery in connection with the illegal export of high-end artificial-intelligence servers to China. The defendants reportedly include employees of Nvidia and Super Micro, placing the case inside companies that make or integrate the constrained hardware rather than only at a distant reseller.

Export-control debates often sound like a contest over lists of chips and destination countries. Enforcement is more granular. Servers can move through intermediaries, paperwork can misstate end users, and employees or contractors may have access to the information needed to route equipment around controls. The credibility of the regime therefore depends on internal sales, logistics, compliance and whistleblower systems.

The allegations are not convictions, and the public reporting does not yet establish how much hardware moved or whether company leadership knew of the conduct. Still, employee-level indictments change the deterrence signal. They tell people inside the supply chain that export restrictions can create personal criminal exposure, not only corporate penalties or canceled shipments.

The case also illustrates Taiwan's increasingly central role in technology enforcement. The island is not merely the manufacturing center for advanced computing. It is a jurisdiction whose investigators and courts can determine whether U.S.-aligned controls work at the point where systems are assembled and shipped.
