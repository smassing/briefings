The Lead · International Security

The opening speeches are the easy part of a difficult U.N. week

The General Assembly has begun amid war with Iran, the continuing invasion of Ukraine, stressed energy markets and heightened concern about artificial intelligence. The meetings matter most when rhetoric turns into an operational change.

Updated September 22 · 4-minute read

President Donald Trump addressed the United Nations General Assembly on Tuesday as the institution confronts a familiar problem in an unusually difficult form: the world’s major conflicts are active, costly and resistant to statements of principle. Associated Press reported that the Iran war, Ukraine, the organization’s financial condition and AI’s social consequences all figured in the day’s agenda.

One immediate sign of the limits was the Iranian delegation’s walkout while Trump described U.S. military action against Tehran. That is not itself a diplomatic development, but it is a useful reminder that speeches and side meetings should not be confused with a process accepted by the parties most able to change a conflict.

The stakes extend beyond the hall. Disruption tied to the Iran conflict has raised pressure on energy and shipping, while Ukraine’s war continues to affect security assistance, refinery capacity and European planning. AI is now a second-order but persistent item: governments recognize that powerful systems cross borders, yet there is still no shared global regime for their safety or accountability.

The useful signals this week will be concrete. Watch for a credible arrangement affecting shipping or sanctions enforcement, an announced diplomatic track with participants and terms, or commitments that move AI governance beyond general principles. Without those, the Assembly will still be valuable as a map of priorities—but not as evidence of resolution.

Courts + Law

Federal Judiciary · Technology Governance

The federal courts are defining an AI program around accountability, not autonomous adjudication

The Judicial Conference’s latest update combines a case-management modernization timetable with a growing AI work program. Its clearest current rule is also the most important: core judicial functions remain human responsibilities.

Published September 17 · 3-minute read

The Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts says its AI task force has identified more than 60 issues, prioritized them and formed seven subject-matter groups. Interim guidance is already in place, with further guidance expected as the work is resolved.

The key boundary is straightforward: courts have been cautioned not to delegate core judicial functions, including decision-making or case adjudication, to AI. Judiciary users remain accountable for work produced with its assistance. That is a sound distinction between using a tool in an administrative or research workflow and transferring authority to it.

The same update puts this work beside the Case Management/Electronic Case Files redesign. The first component of the new system is expected before year-end, with new district-court cases moving to it by the end of 2027. The coupling matters: AI policy is more useful when it arrives with modern systems, security controls and defined ownership rather than as a stand-alone memo.

Identity Security · NIST Guidance

Access tokens deserve the same operational discipline as passwords—and often more

NIST has finalized guidance on protecting online identity and access tokens. The timely lesson is that bearer-style credentials can bypass a carefully designed sign-in experience once they are exposed.

Published September 15 · 3-minute read

NIST’s finalized guidance addresses a familiar but easily underestimated problem: tokens are routinely stored, copied, logged and passed between services because they make modern systems work. An attacker who obtains a valid token may be able to act as its holder without needing to defeat the original authentication control.

The right response is not merely to tell developers to keep secrets secret. Token controls need ownership across their lifecycle: minimal scope and lifetime, protected storage, rotation and revocation paths, careful logging, and detection for anomalous use. These disciplines are especially important for automation and agentic workflows, where credentials may be delegated across tools and environments.

For an enterprise, the practical next step is an inventory of high-value tokens: cloud service principals, API credentials, browser or session artifacts, and tokens issued to automation. Identify where each is created, stored and revoked before a response effort has to reconstruct that information under pressure.

A Focused Review

Test whether a compromised token can be revoked promptly, whether downstream use is visible in logs, and whether an automation workflow receives more privilege or duration than its task requires.

Something Interesting

Measurement Science · Quantum

NIST’s quantum sensors point toward more precise nuclear monitoring

A NIST research effort illustrates a useful pattern in advanced technology: sensitivity at the physical layer can improve verification before software or analytics enters the picture.

Published September 10 · 2-minute read

NIST recently highlighted quantum sensors designed to detect faint signals relevant to nuclear monitoring. The work is not a near-term replacement for established inspection methods. Its interest is in the direction of travel: better sensors can make some measurements possible at lower signal levels, potentially improving what inspectors and researchers can verify.

That kind of progress is easy to overstate. A sensitive instrument is only one part of an operational monitoring system, which also requires calibration, field conditions, trusted procedures and interpretation. But it is a reminder that consequential advances in security and science do not always arrive as a new application; sometimes they begin with a better way to see.
